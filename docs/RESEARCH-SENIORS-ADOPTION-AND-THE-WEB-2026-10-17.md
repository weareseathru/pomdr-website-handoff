# What the evidence says: senior dogs, senior people, how people adopt, and how adults 25 to 85 use the web

**A research synthesis, written 2026-10-17 for Andrew Z.** Sources are primary where one exists (peer-reviewed studies, national polls, the W3C, Pew, AARP, the Grey Muzzle / Maddie's Fund evaluation) and are linked at the end. Where a figure is old, single-shelter, self-reported or advocacy-sourced, it says so. The final sections turn the findings into checks against the site as built, and answer the sitemap question properly, because the honest answer turns out to be about discovery for exactly the visitors the research describes.

---

## 1. Senior dogs in shelters: what is known, and how well

**They wait longer, and the penalty is steep where it is measured.** A study of one shelter's records found seniors stayed about seven days longer than adults, who stayed about five days longer than juveniles. The Grey Muzzle / Maddie's Fund evaluation (Lunghofer, 2020) cites a survey of five open-admission South Carolina shelters where "the average length of stay for a dog over eight years old is three times as long as the length of stay for dogs under eight," and where "50% of senior dogs were euthanized, a rate two-and-a-half times these shelters' typical euthanasia rate." The Oregon Humane Society reported up to three extra days per senior, which across 500 seniors a year "adds up to 1,500 extra days." The oldest national benchmark, ASPCA 2015, put the live-release rate for dogs seven and older at just over half. One Australian open-admission shelter found the opposite (shorter stays for seniors), which its authors attribute to survivorship: only the easy-to-place seniors were kept for rehoming.

**Confidence:** moderate. The studies are single-shelter or grantee-reported; no current national adoption rate by age exists. Shelter Animals Count's 2025 national report gives the dog adoption rate overall as 57 percent of intake (up from 55), 2.8 million dog intakes, longer stays "especially for large dogs," but no age split.

**The welfare consequence is well established:** longer stays raise infectious-disease risk and behavioural decline, and adoptions concentrate in the first week. A 2023 analysis of 27,000 US dogs found dogs who spent at least one night in foster were more than fourteen times as likely to be adopted. For POMDR, which has no shelter and places every dog in a foster home, that is the evidence behind the whole model, and behind making Foster a top-level word in the menu.

**What the Grey Muzzle evaluation found about getting seniors adopted** (71 program directors, 41 volunteers, 13 adopters, 59 organisations, 28 states): the cost of care was "the challenge cited most frequently"; storytelling was the recurring success ("the best way to do that is to tell a story. We take phones out and capture as much of the story as possible so that in three months if we need to raise money, we can recap what happened"); fosters who could "take good photos and share quirky, cute stories" made the difference between a dog who was marketed and one who was not; and the two assumptions grants most often overturned were "euthanasia is the only choice" and that the public would not "open their hearts and homes to older dogs." Grey Muzzle's grantee survey found two-thirds of respondents believe older adults are the most open to adopting senior dogs. One grantee noted barriers in the other direction: retirement communities that bar large dogs, and that "younger seniors (50+) were more interested in our forever foster program."

**What this means for us.** The photo-timeline proposal filed yesterday (POMDR-44) is not a nice-to-have; the only rigorous national study of senior-dog placement names "capture the story as it happens" as the practice that works. And the hospice and sponsorship pages, which fund the "cost of care" barrier directly, deserve the prominence they have.

## 2. Senior people and pets: the Michigan poll

The University of Michigan's National Poll on Healthy Aging is the best population data (2018, adults 50 to 80; 2025, adults 50 and older):

- **55 percent own a pet**, unchanged across the two waves; dogs 68 to 70 percent of owners. Ownership falls with age and with living alone: 64 percent at 50 to 64 versus 46 percent at 65 and over; 60 percent of people living with others versus 43 percent living alone.
- **Benefits owners report:** a sense of purpose (83 percent in 2025), enjoying life (71 to 88 percent), feeling loved (86), stress relief (79), sticking to a routine (62), connecting with others (65), being physically active (64). People in fair or poor health reported more benefit, not less: over 70 percent said their pet helped them cope with physical or emotional symptoms.
- **Barriers:** cost strain has nearly doubled, 18 percent of owners in 2018 to **31 percent in 2025**. Non-owners cite burden (42 percent), cost (23), time (20), allergies (16). The 2025 report adds that ownership "can be especially difficult for people who have other care responsibilities or serious health concerns," and notes housing rules.
- **The poll's own suggestion for people who cannot own:** volunteer at a shelter, pet therapy, pet sitting.

**What this means for us.** Helping Paw answers the two largest barriers in the data (cost and burden) and the "living alone" gap; the site's framing of it as "keep the dog you love" is the right one. Perpetual Care answers the fear the poll implies but does not ask. Foster is the poll's own alternative for people who cannot commit, and the site should keep saying "we cover the costs" early and plainly, because cost is the number that moved. The "seniors for seniors" model has no outcome study behind it (we looked; fee-waiver programs cite general pet-ownership research), so the site should claim the benefits of companionship, which are measured, not the benefits of the programme, which are not.

## 3. How people choose and find a dog

**Why they choose (Weiss, Miller, Mohan-Gibbons, Vela, 2012; 1,491 adopters, five US shelters).** Appearance was the single most-cited reason for dog adopters (26.8 percent for adult dogs, 29 for puppies), then social behaviour toward the adopter, then personality. Age was rated important by 65.6 percent of adult-dog adopters. Adopters valued information from staff or volunteers over cage cards, and valued interacting with the dog over seeing it in a kennel. The internet was cited as an important source by 36 percent of dog adopters. The study has no "senior" category and no adopter ages.

**How online listings work.** Lampe and Witte (2015; 468 dogs) found rated overall photo quality had the largest effect on time to adoption; looking at the camera, standing, an outdoor setting and a sharp image also sped adoption; a bandana or a visible tongue made no difference. An Australian PetRescue study (2020) found partly the reverse (kennel photographs and closed mouths had the shortest stays), which its authors read as adopters responding to a dog who looks in need. A recent survey study found personality descriptions raised perceived adoptability while breed labels lowered it (stated preference, not measured speed).

**How many look online first.** Between a quarter and a half, depending on the study: a third of adopters at one managed-intake shelter had visited Petfinder (Workman and Hoffman, 2015); 48 percent at a Bay Area adoption event had searched websites and 30 percent adopted a pet they saw online (Maddie's Fund, 2013); about 25 percent of all recent pet acquirers researched online (PetSmart Charities / Ipsos). Where dogs come from nationally: APPA 2024 puts shelters at 19 percent and rescues at 14 percent of dogs; AVMA 2025 puts them at 9.4 and 9.7 (different methods; both are approximate). Shelter Animals Count has dogs adopted most often through rescues (32 percent) and government shelters (31).

**Who adopts.** No good age breakdown of *adopters* exists. Pet owners by generation (APPA): Millennials 30 percent, Gen X 25, Boomers 25, Gen Z 20 and rising. POMDR's own range of 25 to 85 is therefore the whole adult population, with the centre of gravity in the 30s to 60s and the people the mission serves at the top.

**What this means for us.** Three things the site already does are confirmed by the evidence: a real photograph, large, before anything else; a story written in the voice of someone who knows the dog (the "staff member or volunteer" adopters trust most); and the dog's personality ahead of its breed. Two things to protect: photo quality on intake (a sharp, outdoor, eye-contact photograph is worth measurable days, and is the cheapest intervention in this document), and the "calm / lap / cuddle" chips on the adopt page, which are personality search, which is what people are actually doing. And since a third to a half of adopters begin online, the site has to be findable, which is section 6.

## 4. How adults 25 to 85 use the web, and what changes with age

**Access is no longer the barrier it was.** Pew's 2025 survey: 78 percent of Americans 65 and over own a smartphone (18 percent in 2013; 42 in 2016; 61 in 2021); 90 percent of 50 to 64; 97 percent of 18 to 29. AARP's 2026 Tech Trends: 90 percent of adults 50-plus own a smartphone and 98 percent of owners use it daily; laptops or desktops are used daily by about 60 percent, tablets by 57. Within the 65-plus group the gradient is steep: in Pew's 2017 data 65 to 69-year-olds went online at twice the rate of the 80-plus (82 versus 44 percent) and owned smartphones at three times the rate (59 versus 17), with income and education mattering as much as age. Older adults are more likely than the young to use a desktop or laptop for reading (34 percent of 65-plus often get news on a computer versus 23 percent of 18 to 29), and less likely to prefer mobile when they have both (44 versus 65 percent).

**Confidence and help.** In Pew's 2017 data, 34 percent of older internet users had little or no confidence using devices for online tasks, and 48 percent said they needed someone to set up or teach them a new device. Nothing newer contradicts this; AARP's reports show usage, not confidence.

**Performance.** Nielsen Norman Group's tests (2002 and later; 44 and then 31 seniors against younger controls) found users 65 and over about 43 percent slower and with task success of 55 percent against 75 for 21 to 55-year-olds; the largest factor was time to comprehend and scan a page. Hart, Chaparro and Halcomb (2008) found the sites most compliant with the NIA/NLM "senior-friendly" guidelines produced higher task success but not better satisfaction or preference, and concluded that guidelines and testing with the actual audience are both needed. The W3C's WAI-AGE literature review (Arch, 2008; 150-plus sources) concluded that existing WCAG guidance already covers most needs of older users, with short-term memory (losing one's place) and separating audio from background noise as the recurring additional needs; the W3C says most of it is still relevant.

**Text size.** A 2022 systematic review of font size for older adults on mobile (Hou et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*) found no universal minimum but a clear direction: older adults prefer larger type regardless of device or typeface; reading speed falls sharply below roughly 9 to 18 arcminutes and again above about 60 to 180; studies that found good results used 14 to 22 pt on tablets at 40 cm (42 to 66 arcminutes), and Yeh (2015) found older adults "struggled even at 14 pt"; increased line spacing helped and increased word spacing hurt (Wang et al., 2009); higher contrast helped (Fujikake et al., 2007); serif versus sans serif was mixed by language and culture. Earlier web studies (Bernard et al., average age 70) found 14-point reliably better than 12 for older readers.

**Touch.** For 65 to 84-year-olds, making standard targets 50 percent larger gave about the same accuracy gain as changing the selection technique (older handheld study); tap error rates for motor-impaired users converge at about 12 mm; screen edges are troublesome; older adults' errors are more often "missed the target entirely" than "slipped off it" (McGill); tremor affects about 6 percent at 60 to 65 and over 20 percent past 95, and causes neighbouring targets to be hit, so spacing matters as much as size.

**What this means for us, and where the site stands against it:**

| Finding | What it asks of a site | POMDR site as built |
|---|---|---|

The gaps are small and known: the Little Green Light frames ignore text size (the strongest single finding of the council, outside our layer), testing with people has not happened, and the two paragraphs the hard-moment pages lack ("what happens in the first 24 hours") are the kind of reassurance the confidence data says older visitors need.

## 5. How this reads against Ronnie's structure

The research keeps returning to the same three design facts, and all three depend on his back office as much as on our pages:

1. **The photograph is the adoption.** Photo quality is the largest measurable lever on time to adoption, and the Grey Muzzle evaluation's strongest practice is capturing a dog's story as it happens. That makes the media pipeline (intake photo standards, the timeline proposal, the R2 import) central, not peripheral, and it makes the public-readiness rule in POMDR-28 (no photo, no listing) a welfare decision as well as a design one: a dog listed with no picture is a dog who waits.
2. **Staff words are the words people trust.** The adopt page's filters and the dog page's story are only as good as what staff enter; the potential-intake flow Ronnie built this month is where those words will be written first. A "story" field with a two-line prompt on the intake form would do more for adoption speed than any layout change left on our list.
3. **Cost is the barrier that is growing.** Thirty-one percent of older owners say a pet strains the budget; the Helping Paw, sponsorship and hospice funding flows, and therefore the donate forms (POMDR-9), are the mechanisms that answer the number.

## 6. The sitemap, answered properly

**Technically:** a sitemap is a list of every address the site has. On this site that list exists in exactly one place, the hourly build (`build/build.php` assembles `page_urls()` plus every dog with a page plus every published person, then renders and pushes to the `pomdr-public` repository that Cloudflare Pages serves). So the sitemap must be written by the build, in the same loop, as one more file in the output, like the `404.html` we already added. The same is true of a `_headers` file for caching and a `_redirects` file for the old addresses: all three must be in the output repo to reach Cloudflare. Ronnie's `AtLaunch.md` plans the redirects as Cloudflare Bulk Redirects in the dashboard instead; either works, and his is the one that survives if the build changes.

**Whose it is:** `build/` is on Ronnie's side of the line we drew in September. We have crossed it twice as pure additions he accepted (the `/team/<slug>/` loop, the `404.html` write), so a sitemap loop is the same shape and about fifteen lines. It is his by courtesy and ours by capability; the agenda ticket (POMDR-45) offers to write all three as additions.

**Why it matters to the people in this document.** Google's own guidance (as relayed by every source we checked; confirm against Search Central) is that a sitemap is unnecessary for a small, well-linked site, *except* for a **new site with few inbound links**, where crawlers may not discover pages for weeks. That is precisely this site on launch day: a new domain's worth of 150 addresses, 80 of them dog pages that change weekly, with inbound links still pointing at the old `.php` and `.html` addresses. The research says a third to a half of adopters begin online and that older adults are more likely to search than to browse; a dog page that Google has not found is a dog page that visitor never sees. For POMDR specifically, each dog page has a short life (weeks), so discovery delay is the difference between being found while the dog is available and being found after. The sitemap with `lastmod` from the build run, plus the `Sitemap:` line in `robots.txt` that is also missing, is the cheapest discovery win on the list; the redirects are the second, because 3,596 adopters have their dog's old address bookmarked (`AtLaunch.md`) and the old site's `dog.php?id=` links are what Google currently ranks.

## 7. What to carry forward

Five things this research changes or confirms about what we do next, in order:

1. **Intake photo standard** (one page for staff and fosters): outdoors or by a window, eye contact, standing, sharp, no cage. It is the single cheapest measured lever on adoption speed and it belongs with the timeline proposal.
2. **No photo, no public listing** (POMDR-28): a welfare rule, not only a design one.
3. **Sitemap and redirects before the DNS moves** (POMDR-17, offered in POMDR-45): discovery is where the online third of adopters start.
4. **Cost language stays early and plain** on Foster, Helping Paw, Hospice and Donate: "we cover the costs" answers the barrier that doubled.
5. **Test with five to eight older visitors** (POMDR-25): every guideline study ends with the same sentence, and so does this one.
