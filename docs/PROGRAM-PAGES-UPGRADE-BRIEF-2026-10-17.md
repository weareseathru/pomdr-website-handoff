# The program pages, taken to the next level: a design brief

**Written 2026-10-17 for Andrew Z.** The question: how would each program page be improved visually, in information layout, in the order it meets a visitor's needs, and in features and wording, to the standard of a nationally recognised nonprofit, while staying what POMDR is: a small, compassionate rescue that runs tight. Nothing here is built. Each page gets a verdict on what to keep, then upgrades ranked by what the visitor feels, with the evidence (the research synthesis, the council, the stress test, the thousand visits) and the cost.

## 0. Start from the mission

The live site states it plainly and the new site should carry it on every program page as its spine:

> *We have a lifetime commitment to all the dogs who come into our care. They will either be adopted into a wonderful, permanent home, or will live out their lives in one of our foster homes.*
> *We help senior citizens pay for veterinary care for their dogs if they are unable to afford medical expenses. We also provide temporary foster care for people who are hospitalized and dog walking for people who are no longer able to walk their dogs.*
> *Our vision is to model lifetime care for dogs and all companion animals, to help bring about a positive change in the way society thinks about and treats senior dogs, and to create better lives for them through rescue, foster, adoption, hospice and education.*

Three design consequences follow and run through everything below:

1. **"Lifetime commitment" is the promise every page should be able to point to.** It is the thing that distinguishes POMDR from a shelter and from most rescues; it belongs in the Why, the dog page, Placing Your Dog, Perpetual Care and Hospice as the same sentence, recognisably, not paraphrased five ways.
2. **Two audiences, one mission: senior dogs and senior people.** The research says the second audience is on a phone, reads slowly, trusts a person over a form, and is increasingly squeezed by cost. Every program page should be readable by someone of 75 in one sitting and should offer a phone number before it offers a form.
3. **"Education" is in the vision and barely on the site.** The national-level organisations teach as well as ask; the Why page is the only teaching page we have. Several upgrades below add teaching where it serves a decision.

**The register to hold:** national-level clarity (one job per page, numbers that are real, a story that moves, an ask that is easy) with small-rescue warmth (a named person, a phone number, the dog's own name in the button, verbatim voices). What we will not borrow from the national sites: pop-ups, urgency timers, upsells, stock photography, "impact calculators" with made-up multipliers, or any claim the live site does not make.

A shared pattern worth adding to the kit, used on four pages below: **the promise strip**, a single quiet band with the lifetime-commitment sentence and a small real photograph, the same on every page it appears, so a visitor learns to recognise it.

## 1. Get help (the hub)

**Who arrives:** a person in a hard week, often older, often on a phone, often not sure which program fits; or an adult child acting for a parent.
**Hierarchy of needs:** 1 reassurance that they are in the right place; 2 a human to talk to; 3 the right door; 4 what happens next.
**Keep:** the four "I want…" cards in the visitor's words; the phone in the band; "Not sure which? Contact us."

**Upgrades**
1. **A person, not only a number.** Under the band's Call button: "You will reach Shanel or Alejandra, our Helping Paw team, Monday to Saturday" (names and days from staff; they are already on the team page). The research is unambiguous that older visitors trust a named person over a form. Cost: one line, once staff confirm who answers.
2. **Spanish from the first screen.** A small "En español" link in the band, leading to the Spanish application page and a short Spanish version of these four cards. Today Spanish appears only two pages deep. Half a day with a Spanish reader.
3. **"If today is the hard day" strip** under the cards: the three things that cannot wait (a dog alone after a hospital admission, a dog whose owner has died, a dog that must leave a home this week) each with the single next step and the number. The council's Margaret and the thesis both asked for "what happens in the first 24 hours"; this is where it lives. Content from staff; layout is the existing card grid.
4. **The promise strip** at the foot: the lifetime-commitment sentence, so the person placing a dog knows the end of the story before they begin.

## 2. Helping Paw

**Who arrives:** a senior or a caregiver facing a vet bill or a dog they cannot walk; a hospice social worker; a donor checking what the money does.
**Hierarchy:** 1 am I eligible; 2 what exactly can I get; 3 how do I ask, in English or Spanish; 4 who else this has helped.
**Keep:** the band, the new opening, the three kinds of help, the folded questions, the purple close.

**Upgrades**
1. **Eligibility before everything.** Move the two eligibility answers (counties; income rules for financial help) out of the fold and into a two-column "Is this for me?" block right after the first split, in plain words: *"You live in Monterey County (or Santa Cruz or San Benito, as funds allow). For money toward a vet bill, you receive Social Security or disability, are out of work, or live on an income at or below the poverty line."* The folded version stays for detail. This is the single most important fact on the page and it is currently the fourth click.
2. **Real numbers, if staff have them.** A small numbers band: dogs walked, grants given, households helped last year. The vision promises education and the national sites prove impact with figures; Dana flagged their absence. Content from POMDR (the council's content gap 1); layout exists.
3. **One client voice, with a face.** The testimonials page holds 36 Helping Paw voices; the live site featured "just one of the many households we've been able to help." A `voice-feature` with a photograph and one verbatim quote above the three cards. Content exists; permission for the photo from staff.
4. **Volunteer the other way.** A quiet card at the end: "Could you walk a dog for someone who cannot?" linking to the Volunteer role. The page serves two audiences and currently speaks to one.
5. **Wording:** rename the Spanish button to "Solicitar ayuda" (ask for help) if staff agree; "Solicitar en español" names the language, not the act.

## 3. Placing your dog (surrender)

**Who arrives:** an owner in distress, or their family, often ashamed; sometimes a social worker.
**Hierarchy:** 1 you will not be judged; 2 will you take my dog, and under what conditions; 3 what happens to the dog; 4 the form, or a human.
**Keep:** the new opening, the four photo steps, "what we can and cannot take on", the two cross-links, the close.

**Upgrades**
1. **The promise strip after the steps**: "Once your dog is ours, they are ours for life." It is the live site's own commitment and the thing that lets a person let go. Today it is a clause inside step four.
2. **What to expect in the first week**, as three short lines under step one: we reply within N days, we will ask to meet your dog, you can change your mind until the day. (N from staff.) The thesis and the council both asked for this.
3. **Say who you will talk to.** "You will talk with Courtney, our Adoptions Coordinator" or whoever handles intake, from the roster.
4. **A gentler form page.** The intake questionnaire page should open with the same "no judgement" sentence and the phone number above the frame; it is the page a crying person lands on. One paragraph.
5. **Photographs:** the step photos are 85px thumbnails; one of them should be a real surrendered dog in their foster home, larger, with a line about where they are now (a happy tail), because the fear is "what becomes of them." Content from staff.

## 4. Perpetual Care

**Who arrives:** a planner, usually 65-plus, often with an attorney or adult child; a worrier at 2 a.m.
**Hierarchy:** 1 is this real and will you really take my dog; 2 what does it cost; 3 how does it work legally; 4 how do I start.
**Keep:** the headline (Margaret: "exactly my question"), the four steps, the seven questions, the NPR link, the number in the button.

**Upgrades**
1. **A one-page printable.** This is the page people take to a lawyer or a daughter. A "Print this page" action and a print stylesheet that keeps the steps, the questions and the number, and drops the chrome. The thesis listed the print check as owed; this is the page to start with.
2. **The attorney's paragraph.** A short block addressed to the professional ("For attorneys and trustees"): what the trust should name, how to reach us, what we need in the Pet Profile. Teaching, in the vision's sense, for the person who will actually draft the document. Content from Patrick Casey (the advisory council's legal consultant) or staff.
3. **A real enrolled-dog story.** One `voice-feature`: a dog who came to POMDR through the program and where they are now, in the foster's or adopter's words. The strongest proof the promise is kept. Content from staff.
4. **"Questions people ask us"** could open the first two answers by default (old or ill dog; what if I die) since they are the two fears; the rest stay folded.
5. **The promise strip** here is the page's own sentence; no second one needed.

## 5. Fostering, Become a foster, Dogs needing a foster home

**Who arrives:** someone with a spare room and a soft heart, often retired; a past foster checking who needs them; a renter unsure if they qualify.
**Hierarchy:** 1 who needs me right now; 2 what it costs me and what you provide; 3 what support I get; 4 the application.
**Keep:** the voices with portraits (the best thing on these pages), the three steps, the embedded application, the truthful "you provide the food" lead.

**Upgrades**
1. **Dogs first on the Fostering page.** The page sells fostering for 1,500 words before showing a dog. A strip of the current foster-needed dogs (the same `pet-card`s, three or four, "and N more") directly under the band, with "See all" to the list. The thousand-visit test found the foster journey's only real friction was reaching the list.
2. **Short-term and long-term, said plainly.** Ronnie's admin now distinguishes temporary from long-term foster need. The list page should group them: "Needs a home for a few weeks" (hospital stays, Helping Paw) and "Needs a home until adopted," each with its own one-line promise, because the commitment is different and many people can say yes to the first who cannot to the second. Our template; his data.
3. **A foster FAQ** (the kit's `qa`): do I need a yard; can I foster if I rent; what if it does not work out; who pays the vet; can I adopt my foster. Every answer is on the live site or in the foster voices already; eight questions, half a day.
4. **The mentor, named.** "Every foster gets a mentor" lands harder as "Rhonda has mentored 40 fosters" (if true; from staff).
5. **Become a foster:** the band should carry the one-sentence truth the research supports: *"A dog who spends one night in a foster home is fourteen times more likely to be adopted."* That is a measured figure (2023 analysis of 27,000 US dogs, cited in the synthesis) and the most persuasive sentence available; confirm the citation and use it once.

## 6. Volunteer

**Who arrives:** a retiree with time, a student with hours to log, a professional with a skill, a family.
**Hierarchy:** 1 is there something I can actually do; 2 how much time; 3 what it is like; 4 apply.
**Keep:** the role cards, the party split, the mosaic, the numbers band.

**Upgrades**
1. **Time on every card.** Each role card gets a line: "Two hours, weekday mornings" or "An hour once a month" or "From home, any hours." Time is the first question and the cards answer only the task. From staff; a dozen words each.
2. **"Start here" roles.** Mark the two or three roles that need no training as "Good first job" with a small chip; the council noted ten equal cards give no place to start.
3. **A volunteer voice with a face**, as on Fostering: one `voice-feature` from the 34 foster-and-volunteer testimonials, with a photograph.
4. **The party as proof of belonging:** keep the mosaic but add the year's headcount to the caption if known ("2024: 310 people and 90 dogs"). From staff.
5. **Tidy the grid:** ten cards in three columns leave one alone; either a tenth that is really two (split "Your specialty" into "Writing and design" and "Photography and video") or two columns. Design decision, ten minutes.

## 7. Donate

**Who arrives:** a monthly giver, a one-time giver moved by a dog, a memorial giver, a planner, a company.
**Hierarchy:** 1 where does it go; 2 how much and how often; 3 memorial and long-term; 4 trust (tax ID, reports).
**Keep:** the new band photograph and opening, the tax line, the impact-report link, the grouped "other ways."

**Upgrades**
1. **Make the chips true** (POMDR-9): each amount and frequency opens a form that already says so; until then, remove the chips, because a control that does nothing teaches the donor the page is cosmetic (Dana).
2. **"What a gift does," in real units.** Three tiles: what a dental costs, what a month of a hospice dog's medication costs, what a Helping Paw grant is capped at ($500 is already on the site). Real figures from the clinic; no invented multipliers. This is the national-level pattern done honestly.
3. **A plain tribute path first:** "Give in memory or in honor; we send a card" ahead of plaques and tags, with a dedication field on the form.
4. **Monthly as the quiet default.** The national sites make monthly the first option because it is what sustains a rescue; a single line under the chips, "Most of our dogs are cared for by people who give a little every month," with Monthly selected first, if LGL allows.
5. **The 2024 impact report as a card** with its cover and two headline numbers, where the bulleted link is now.
6. **Sponsor a dog** promises names on the dog's page; make it true (Ronnie, item 2.6 of the companion proposal) and show the sponsored dogs on this page with their sponsors' first names.

## 8. Events

**Who arrives:** a local wanting to meet dogs this weekend; a past adopter; a supporter looking for the gala.
**Hierarchy:** 1 what is on this week and where; 2 can I just show up; 3 what happened recently; 4 how to hear next time.
**Keep:** "No appointment, and nobody will ask you to take a dog home"; the two groups; the new address and map link.

**Upgrades**
1. **Which dogs will be there.** The live site's adoption events had no dog list; the Hub could. An event linked to the dogs attending (Ronnie's events table plus a join) turns "an adoption event" into "come meet Pie and Ahi Tuna." The single strongest reason to attend. His schema; our card.
2. **A "Recently" strip**: two or three past events with a photograph and a line ("Harvest Festival: 14 dogs came, 3 found homes"). Needs event photos (POMDR-10) and counts from staff.
3. **Add to calendar** links (plain `.ics`, no script) on each event.
4. **The gala and fundraisers** deserve their own card style with a date held well ahead, since the fundraisers column reads as empty between events.
5. **News into Events** (from the archive's 13 items), so the page is never a single entry.

## 9. Hospice and Sponsor a dog

**Who arrives:** a giver moved by a particular dog; a past sponsor; someone who cannot adopt but wants to help one dog.
**Hierarchy:** 1 who are they; 2 what does sponsoring do; 3 how; 4 thanks.
**Keep:** the five dogs with their cards; "No dog is ever left behind"; the sponsor-page name line.

**Upgrades**
1. **Each hospice dog's page carries a "What sponsorship covers this month" line** (medication, a specialist visit) in staff's words, and the sponsors' first names. The timeline proposal makes this routine.
2. **Hospice page: the promise strip** is the page's reason; one real story of a dog who lived out their days in POMDR care, with the foster's words, would do more than the paragraph.
3. **Sponsor levels in plain words** (a week of medication, a month of care) rather than amounts alone, if the clinic can price them honestly.

## 10. Why a senior dog, and the teaching the vision asks for

**Who arrives:** a first-time adopter deciding between a puppy and a senior; a sceptical family member; a teacher or journalist.
**Keep:** the three reasons, the worries answered, the quotes on the wall, the lifetime-commitment paragraph.

**Upgrades**
1. **Name the lifetime commitment as a reason to adopt**, not only to surrender: "If it ever does not work out, the dog comes back to us, for life." It removes the adopter's largest fear and it is already POMDR's promise.
2. **The honest medical paragraph.** The research names vet-cost fear as the top barrier to adopting seniors; the live site answers it with "we are transparent." Go one step further: what every dog has had before adoption (exam, senior blood panel, dental, the fee range on the process page) in one plain block. All of it is already on the site, scattered.
3. **A short "Senior dogs 101"** section (the vision's "education"): what counts as senior by size, what changes, what does not. Three paragraphs, staff-checked, and the page becomes the one a journalist links to.
4. **Share cards.** This is the page most worth sharing; it should have its own `og:image` with a face and the line "The best dogs are sometimes the oldest."

## 11. Cross-cutting, in priority order

1. **The promise strip** as a kit pattern, on Get help, Placing Your Dog, Hospice and Why (half a day, one partial).
2. **Named people** on the hard-moment pages (one line each, from the roster).
3. **Time and eligibility said first**, on Volunteer and Helping Paw.
4. **Real figures** where staff have them (Helping Paw, clinic costs, events), never where they do not.
5. **One verbatim voice with a face per program page**, from the 152 testimonials and the foster voices already on the site.
6. **Spanish within one tap** of Get help.
7. **A print stylesheet checked on Perpetual Care first.**
8. **Dogs at the top of the foster pages**, and which dogs at each event.

## 13. What stays small

No pop-ups, no countdowns, no "only 3 left," no donation nudges in the chrome beyond the one Donate pill, no stock images, no chatbot, no login walls, no third line of buttons. The whole brief adds one kit pattern, several one-line facts, a handful of real voices, and asks the back office for two joins. The site stays forty pages and one stylesheet, and sounds like the people at 615 Forest Avenue.
